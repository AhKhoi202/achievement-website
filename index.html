My Resume

True financial literacy isn't born in lecture halls; it is forged from counting every single coin, mapping out every investment, and solving real problems for real people

The relentlessly blaring horn just stopped; the rumble of heavy trucks kept vibrating, causing the tremor to even widely spread to the second floor of a small 2 storey house in Viet Nam.

Because my house is near a hectic highway, it is irritating to concentrate on doing one's business.

Since I was a 4-year-old child, my dad has been really cool. He always bought a lot of intriguing stuff for me, such as commercial airplane LEGO, 1000 questions that kids might ask, repair tools, and 6 essential piggy banks; however, I have to do a trade for what I love. Every piece of toy has to be paid for by chores' hour or money from cleaning shoes. A desirable toy costs 2 weeks of working, so it means that if working 7 days a week isn't enough, and to ease my eagerness I have to find a shortcut. I have 3 main goals for saving money: 1 - money for sharing; 2 - money for spending; 3 - money for saving. The money that I can use to buy things is the savings; however, I still have to fill out a withdrawal form for my mom. This money is all lucky money, and I was prohibited from using it for personal joy, but for academic purposes. Therefore, I have to work it out by asking for a commitment to loan me money to work at a certain level in academic performance rather than doing chores for 7 days. In the end, I only have to work for 7 days and have my toys.

When I turned 9, my younger sister was 6; we all had lots of lucky money after the Tet holiday. With an huge amount of money, my parents afraid of bad dignity and low financial literacy, so they come up to urge us opening a business. People making drinks from fruit were really satisfied, and everyone during that time loved the "healthy" in terms of food. We sat down and whispered together for a while, listening to my dad; we started to design a mind map of our idea. Unfortunately, the capital is 2 million VND, but the tools cost 38% of the total money, ~ 760k. Then we have to ask our parents for an investment, this is my first contract of loan. After all the capital, there are still 5 brands of straw, 10 types of cups and caps, and basic ingredient suppliers: orange, sugar, honey, kumquat, waiting to be compared and decided. While figuring out the source is hard, because of selling for my dad's colleagues only, it turned out to be a closed loop business tricky. Plus, I was too generous with our ingredients. If one day we don't have enough buyers, vice versa, we will end up broke or lack of juice to sell. Thanks to startup privilege, my parents freed me from the financial burden of electricity and land. 3 months of summer, our profit was 1 million, which could be seen as a failure case, but it taught me about targeting the true customers and managing our money by writing on ceiling paper to track our profit and loss. Later, I attended an abacus class, where I explored my potential in learning math and proving my ability to adapt. I joined a competition and successfully made it to the last round after 4 months of studying.

In 2020, when my mother gave birth to my younger brother, my paternal grandparents introduced me to Chinese chess. My grandfather, an avid player, often took me to meet his chessmates while babysitting. Soon, I was competing against elderly players, enduring sarcastic remarks like, “Are you sure?” After more than 50 matches, failure became my closest teacher. I even lost 5 consecutive games and my favorite snack to my granduncle, a master who later rewarded my persistence with encouragement and valuable strategies. These experiences taught me to predict opponents’ moves, calculate 4 steps ahead (though I could only manage 2), create deceptive patterns, and remain calm under pressure. Chinese chess has strengthened my analytical thinking and strategic visualization. Beyond the chessboard, these skills help me plan family trips precisely, making me our unofficial travel planner. Most importantly, every defeat has taught me to learn from mistakes and adapt my next move.

Confidence is not born on the stage; it is built through every fear faced, every failure embraced, and every lesson learned

1. Overcoming Shyness

During puberty, separating from old friends made me less communicative. Entering secondary school, I became a shy girl, so my dad encouraged me to take MC lessons. My first class was embarrassing: I practiced voice acting and vocal modulation online with 30 students, most of them 20 years older than me. I learned to control pitch and intensity to engage audiences.

2. First Achievement: Green Minecraft

Meanwhile, my technology teacher invited me to join the Green Minecraft competition team. For the first time, we reached the Top 15 nationwide, giving me confidence to pursue new challenges.

3. My First Stage

My school selected me to present at its quarterly seminar because I had presentation training and project leadership experience. Standing before 50 parents with a microphone and screen was intimidating. Although my teacher couldn't attend, he teased me about shaking onstage. Determined to prove him wrong, I presented naturally and used humor to make the audience laugh. Afterward, a father approached my dad and offered me my first potential career opportunity.

4. Discovering My Passion

That experience inspired me to pursue more public-speaking opportunities. I became one of my class's most engaging presenters and frequently spoke at family events. Although I initially hesitated when friends invited me to competitions, after the fourth invitation, I gradually developed a passion for presenting and representing younger relatives.

5. Learning from Failure

My journey wasn't without setbacks. In the Blue Ocean business competition, my team's proposal failed the entry round because it lacked financial viability and real-world market validation. This taught me that an engaging pitch may attract attention, but only a sound financial model and sustainable unit economics can keep a business alive.

With colors, I create worlds; through a lens, I discover them

Admittedly, I'm not a talented kid in my childhood. The kindergarten is a place to tell me that truth. In a drawing lesson, every kid has their fine pictures capturing an activity of culture while a distorted fat stickman which I got wrong from what my mom taught me is the only thing I have on the vast canvas. In a moment, my teacher pointed out a girl's nice-oriented picture of a woman who is driving a boat inside a coconut river; my eyes filled up with awe and jealousy. I asked my dad to buy me a new pack of crayons to practice crazily. Besides, my mom just only taught me to draw a few details of life and nature; therefore, I searched online for tutorials from different sources like 7 color sister, POPSKIDS, VTV 7 from Youtube to Tv shows. Fortunately, my parent startup their company and left a lot of unused draft and abundance paper, which is an awesome chance of paper resources for my very first stroke. This paper turned into my favourite area for painting, sketching, and idealizing my imagination. After 2 years of practicing, my favourite characters from TV cartoons can properly stay on that paper, like the Coward Dog, Ben 10, and Doraemon. My parents realized my hobby and brought me more specialized paper and color. From that and so on, my skills of drawing went from basic shapes to more distorted shapes but somehow better-looking characters with easily recognized details. Throughout the journey, I became a main drawer and artist in the school activities of my class and became an art teacher for my relatives and my siblings as well. To improve my academic performance, I use my imagination in studying with the mind map method. It allows me to add aesthetic things into tedious formulas and sneak over without any punishments from my teachers for being distracted in class.

My interest in visual storytelling naturally expanded into photography through my father's collection of 3 cameras. He taught me to observe details, adjust focus, control patterns, and capture family moments and landscapes.

During a trip to Ca Mau, while leaning over a muddy riverbank to photograph sunset, I accidentally dropped an unsecured camera lens into the mud because of my easy-thinking brain, which put it in my pocket instead of a secured bag. The cover and camera are $ 1,157; with the soft alluvial terrain, it'll subside anything that is heavy above it. I tried for the first time and noticed its weak structure, so I traumatizingly turned to ask some boatman to come over. He is a local, and we were thinking about a solution and he came up with an idea of taking a thin piling to stick the cover to the mud and hold it up. Ultimately, the lens was saved, but the scare taught me a lasting lesson in taking responsibility for borrowed stuff and preserving a double-check for any over-optimistic idea. From practicing drawing and the dropped camera's cover in the mud, artistic pursuits reinforced my core operational philosophy of recycling the same functional tools, and the muddy lens in Ca Mau taught me a fundamental principle of risk management: to protect a bigger thing, it always begins with adhering to the simplest protocols, even one as small as a lens cap.

A short quote about growing up and a promising future (after age 15)

From a girl counting over her toys, operating her generous fruit stand without precise calculations of profit, to a presenter, a leader of several activities at school and international events, I learned essential elements of life: patience, courage, confidence, assertiveness, and love of humans. Without my family, my friends, my teachers or the local man in Ca Mau, I would never grow up well. In the future, the ability to help out more and more people will become my mission. Not only do I continue to teach my relatives, but also create an environment for Vietnamese kids to improve inside out in financial literacy too.
